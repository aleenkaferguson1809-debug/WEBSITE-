01. The Abstract

What this project actually does

My project is an AI-powered smart recycling bin that uses machine learning image recognition to detect whether an item is a recyclable bottle or general waste (chip packet), then physically sorts it into the correct bin using servo motors, with real-time feedback through LED lights, sound, and a live AI confidence score.

I chose this project because recycling contamination is a real problem: people often put the wrong items in the wrong bin, either by accident or uncertainty. I wanted to remove that guesswork and make correct sorting automatic.

Key challenges included training the AI to reliably tell bottles and chip packets apart under different lighting and angles, wiring multiple components without conflicts, and syncing camera detection with servo movement in real time.

The problem
Recyclable items end up in general waste (and vice versa) simply because people don't know which bin something belongs in, or don't take the time to check.
The approach
Remove human error from the decision entirely: a camera and AI model decide, servo motors act, and the outcome is logged automatically.
The result
A working prototype that classifies an item, sorts it mechanically, and counts every sort, turning one bin into a measurable sustainability habit.
Did You Know?

Why incorrect sorting is a real problem

Incorrectly sorted rubbish is called contamination, items placed in the wrong bin, like chip packets, plastic bags, or food waste ending up in recycling. Contamination makes recycling harder, more expensive, and sometimes impossible to process.

75.6M
tonnes of waste generated in Australia in 2022–23, including 3.0 million tonnes of plastic
22M
tonnes of that waste went straight to landfill instead of being recovered
~14%

11M+
tonnes of plastic enter the world's oceans every year, harming marine environments (UNEP)

What contamination costs

When recyclable materials are rejected because of contamination, useful materials like plastic, cardboard, and metal are wasted instead of being made into new products, increasing the need for new raw materials, which creates more pollution and energy use.

Contamination can also damage sorting equipment and create safety risks for workers. Sustainability Victoria explains that contaminants are items placed in the wrong bin, and they can damage processing equipment or risk worker safety.

Why plastic specifically

Plastic waste is especially harmful because it can last in the environment for a very long time. If plastic rubbish isn't sorted or managed properly, it can break into microplastics and enter waterways, oceans, and ecosystems.

Waste sent to landfill also contributes to climate change, organic waste breaking down underground produces landfill gas made mostly of methane and carbon dioxide, and methane traps much more heat than CO₂ over a 100-year period.

The Process

From hand to bin in eight steps

Every sort runs through three connected systems, a website doing the seeing and thinking, a microbit doing the moving, and a second microbit doing the counting. Here's the full chain, in the order it actually fires.

01 📷
Camera scans the object
A Logitech webcam captures a live feed of the item the moment it's placed in the bin.
02 🧠
AI classifies it
A Google Teachable Machine model decides: plastic bottle, or chip packet, with a live confidence score.
03 🌐
Website confirms the result
The result has to hold steady above a confidence threshold first, so a flickering guess can't trigger the sort.
04 🔌
Command sent over USB
The GitHub-hosted site sends "BOTTLE" or "CHIPS" to microbit 1 via Web Serial.
05 ⚙️
Servo swings the paddle
microbit 1 drives a GeekServo motor, angling the paddle toward the correct chute.
06 ↩️
Paddle resets to centre
Gravity does the rest, dropping the item into its bin while the paddle re-centres for the next one.
07 📡
Wireless message fires
microbit 1 radios "RECYCLE" or "RUBBISH" to microbit 2, no wires between the two units.
08 🔢
Counter ticks up
microbit 2's four-digit display increases the running total, a live usage record for the school.
Under the Hood

Three connected devices, one decision

The system is really three small computers talking to each other over three different connections: USB serial, microbit radio, and the chute itself. No single part does everything; each one hands off to the next.

DEVICE 0

The Website (GitHub Pages)

Hosts the Teachable Machine AI model and shows the camera feed, the predicted class, and the live confidence percentage as it scans.

  • Holds a result steady before accepting it, to avoid reacting to a flickering guess
  • Sends the final decision to microbit 1 over a USB Web Serial connection
USB SERIAL → "BOTTLE" / "CHIPS"
DEVICE 1

microbit + Boson board + GeekServo

The main sorting controller. Receives the command, drives the servo to the matching side of the chute, then returns it to centre. Also runs the motion sensor and LED strip.

  • Bottle → moves toward the recycling chute
  • Chips → moves toward the general-rubbish chute
  • One small motor steering a paddle, gravity does the lifting, not the servo
MICROBIT RADIO → "RECYCLE" / "RUBBISH"
DEVICE 2

microbit + Grove Shield + 4-digit display

The sustainability dashboard. Every successful sort received over radio adds one to the running total, shown live on the display. Also reads the ultrasonic ranger and rotation knob.

  • No wired connection to the sorting unit, fully wireless handoff
  • Turns sorting events into a measurable usage record for the school

One entry point, one sloped chute, one paddle deciding the side. Built and wired inside a cardboard housing as the prototype casing.

The physical build, running: micro:bit, LED strip, and battery pack mounted on the cardboard housing.
02. Inspiration & 03. Problem Statement

Why I built this

The idea for this project was inspired by my current school topics about sustainability and my elective: Emerging Technologies. STEM is my passion, and Albert Park College is heavily focused on sustainability. I decided to embrace my learning of AI and coding from Emerging Technologies class and worked with my STEM skills to create the final idea.

Within my high school, I've encountered unclean learning areas after lunch and recess. Kids are too lazy to place their rubbish in the correct bin, so this way they put their rubbish through one hole that helps recycle. I noticed that even well-intentioned people often sort their rubbish incorrectly, which leads to contaminated recycling loads that end up in landfill anyway.

I wanted to explore whether combining AI vision technology with simple embedded electronics could make correct sorting automatic, rather than relying on people to know or remember the rules.

Problem statement
Many recyclable items end up in general waste (and vice versa) simply because people don't know which bin something belongs in, or don't take the time to check. This reduces the effectiveness of recycling programs and increases the amount of recoverable material that ends up in landfill.
My aim
Remove human error from the sorting process by using AI to make the decision automatically and consistently, every time, without needing anyone to read a label first.
04–07. Design & Key Features

What's actually inside the bin

My machine uses a Logitech webcam to capture a live image of an item placed in the bin. This feed is processed by an AI model trained in Google Teachable Machine, which classifies the item as either a bottle or a chip packet. This classification is sent to 2 micro:bits, which is a vital part of the prototype as it controls a servo motor to physically direct the item into the correct bin, while an LED and buzzer give the user instant visual and audio confirmation.

microbit 2 wired to the 4-digit counter and rotation knob, mounted inside the housing.
LED Strip

Changes colour depending on what was detected, green for recyclable, red for general waste, giving instant visual feedback.

Ultrasonic Ranger

Measures the distance to the rubbish already inside the bin to estimate how full it is, so it can be emptied before overflowing.

Motion Sensor

Detects when someone approaches and automatically powers the system on, so it isn't running constantly.

2 Servos

Physically rotate a flap/chute to direct the item into the correct bin once a classification is received, turning the AI's decision into physical action.

Teachable Machine

Google's browser-based tool used to train the image classification model on photos of bottles and chip packets.

TinkerCAD

Used to plan and prototype the design and circuitry digitally before building the physical machine.

MakeCode

Programmed in MakeCode (and later JavaScript) to coordinate the two micro:bits and their sensors.

Rotation Knob

Shows how confident the model is of its classification. Sets the minimum confidence needed before the machine acts, cutting down on wrong sorts.

How It Solves the Problem

Technology doing the deciding, not people

This AI rubbish sorter helps address the contamination problem by using technology to identify rubbish before it goes into the wrong bin. Instead of relying only on people to make the correct decision, the system uses a camera, AI model, micro:bits, sensors, and motors to automatically guide items into the correct section.

This can help: reduce recycling contamination · stop recyclable bottles going into general rubbish · prevent non-recyclable rubbish from entering recycling · make waste sorting easier for users · support better recycling habits through automation.

Why This Isn't Just a Sign on a Bin

Most "smart bins" stop at a label.
This one acts.

A lot of school sustainability projects identify waste and display an instruction. This system is the rarer kind: it makes the decision, executes it mechanically, and logs the outcome, without a person needing to read or trust a label at all.

CREATIVITY

A pinball machine, repurposed

The sorting mechanism borrows directly from pinball paddles: gravity moves the object, and a small servo only has to nudge a lightweight divider, not lift or carry the item's full weight.

AI UNDERSTANDING

Confidence over guesswork

The AI model (Google Teachable Machine) doesn't get to act on a single lucky frame, its prediction must stay above a confidence threshold for a sustained period before the website trusts it enough to move the servo.

DIFFICULTY

Three systems, two protocols

A browser-based AI model, two microbits, and a servo all have to agree with each other across two different communication protocols, USB Web Serial and microbit radio, to complete one sort.

IMPACT

Turns one bin into a data point

The four-digit counter means the bin doesn't just sort waste, it produces a usage number a school can actually track over a term, turning a one-off invention into ongoing sustainability data.

08. Challenges

What actually got in the way

01Coding at a new level

The main challenge was my level of coding and programming knowledge. Before this build, I'd always been learning how to code and program robotic components, but never at this level of complexity. Through this project I taught myself how to program a micro:bit using Python and how to build and host a website through GitHub. Both things I'd never done before. It was a steep learning curve, but it turned into one of the most valuable parts of the project, as I came out of it able to use Python for both my website and my micro:bit code.

02Limited resources

A smaller challenge was working within the resources available to me. I would have liked access to a second ultrasonic sensor and a more powerful servo capable of handling heavier loads. My school provided a solid amount of resources to work with, but I believe the project could perform even better with more equipment, allowing the bin to handle a more realistic volume and weight of rubbish.

03Physically installing

Physically installing the electronics was another challenge. I had to fit the micro:bits and all their components neatly inside a cardboard housing while keeping the final build looking clean and presentable. I enjoyed this part of the process, but the micro:bit's small components sometimes needed to be unplugged and reconnected, likely due to calibration issues, and the code occasionally needed to be re-downloaded after periods of use to keep it running properly. So the challenge wasn't just mounting the parts securely, it was also keeping the programming reliable throughout testing.

04The AI recognising the bin itself

A massive problem I ran into was the AI recognising the cardboard background of the bin as an object of its own. Because the training photos were taken with the cardboard visible behind the item, Teachable Machine started picking up on the box itself rather than just the bottle or chip packet, which threw off classifications even when nothing had actually changed. To fix this I had to change the background used behind the scan zone and go back and adjust the code so the model wouldn't treat the cardboard as a recognisable object, retraining it to focus only on the item being held up to the camera.

09. What I Would Do Next Time

Building it again, smarter

If I were to do this project again, I would spend more time planning the full design from the start instead of adapting the build around whatever materials I had on hand. I initially coded and set up only one servo motor, then had to go back and add the second once I realised both were needed to properly direct items into either bin. Something better upfront planning would have avoided.

I'd also like to replace the cardboard housing with a 3D-printed casing, as it would look neater and hold the components more securely than cardboard does. My school doesn't currently have a 3D printer, so cardboard was the most realistic option available to me, but it's the first thing I'd upgrade given the chance.

10. Conclusion

Does it do what I set out to do?

My project successfully achieves what I set out to accomplish: it accurately detects whether an item is a recyclable bottle or general-waste chip packet, and automatically sorts it into the correct bin without needing the user to make that decision themselves.

This demonstrates that combining accessible AI tools like Teachable Machine with simple embedded electronics is a genuinely effective way to reduce recycling contamination, turning a everyday school bin into a small but real piece of sustainability infrastructure.
